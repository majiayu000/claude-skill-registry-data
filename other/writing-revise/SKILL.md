# Revise

Change the substance while holding the commitments fixed. Where
`writing-edit` touches sentences with meaning fixed, this touches meaning and
shows its work.

1. **Establish the target.** The `writing-critique` findings, or the user's
   brief. If neither exists and the change is structural, run
   `writing-critique` first — revision without a diagnosis degenerates into
   taste-based rewriting, and the user loses the thread of why anything moved.
2. **Inventory the commitments** before touching anything: facts, numbers,
   names, exact terms, promises to the reader, qualifiers, the audience, and —
   for fiction — established canon in the bible (facts, timeline, voice tells,
   who-knows-what). These are protected; a revision may not quietly change
   them. Inventing a fact to bridge a gap is the one unforgivable revision.
3. **Revise from the top down**: spine, then structure and scene order, then
   paragraphs, then sentences. A lower-tier fix is wasted when a higher-tier
   change relocates it — fix the spine, then re-read, then the next tier.
4. **Surface conflicts, never bridge them.** When a needed fix would change a
   commitment, remove a condition, or require material you don't have, stop and
   present the conflict with its tradeoff. Never invent a fact, source, or
   scene to bridge a gap; a new scene or section is proposed, not smuggled.
5. **Re-read the file from disk before each write.** Edit in place; never
   overwrite text the user has changed. If the user edited a passage since you
   read it, their edit outranks the plan — re-plan around it.
6. **Return a change log** keyed to the findings or brief: what moved, what was
   cut, what was added, and any commitment renegotiated. A revision the user
   cannot audit is a revision they cannot trust.

Handoffs: line polish afterwards is `writing-edit`; new material that changes
the canon goes through `writing-bible` so the ledger stays true.
