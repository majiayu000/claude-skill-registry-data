# Editorial voice (WSJ-like)

Write like a careful business reporter explaining numbers to a busy, sceptical
reader: precise, attributed, short, and honest about what is not known.

This is a WSJ-like news style: our own adaptation, written from cited
public sources (the front-page nut-graf formula, Bill Blundell's story
blocks, reported 2023 usage changes) in our own words. It is not The Wall
Street Journal's style guide, is not affiliated with or endorsed by the
paper, and uses none of its fonts or logos. See `SOURCES.md`.

## Structure

**Briefs and monitoring updates: inverted pyramid.** The most important fact
first, with its number and comparison. Then the next most important. A
reader who stops after one paragraph has the news.

**Analyses and exception reports: lede, nut graf, body, kicker.** This is the
front-page feature formula the Journal is known for, which Poynter traces to
managing editor Barney Kilgore's 1941 ban on "today" and "yesterday" in leads:
news need not have happened today to matter, so lead with significance and
trends rather than the calendar.

1. **Lede (paragraph 1).** A specific, concrete opening: the fact, the
   customer case, the campaign. Not the calendar ("This week, …") and not the
   process ("We analysed …"). In a business briefing the lede is usually the
   number itself; an anecdote (one customer's cancelled order) is used only
   when it stands for the pattern, and says so.
2. **Nut graf (right after the lede; by paragraph 2–4 at the latest).** One
   paragraph that steps back from the case to the pattern and answers "why
   should I bother with this?": what changed, how big, why now, what it means
   for a decision. Our own practice (not from the cited sources): write it
   first, as one or two sentences, before drafting, and rewrite it as the
   evidence comes in, because it exposes gaps. It is an
   argument, so every clause in it needs evidence in the body. One paragraph;
   two or three at the very most. If you cannot write it, there is no story
   yet: send a monitoring update instead.
3. **Body: cover the bases, support then counter-evidence.** Build the body
   from the six blocks below, in the order the reader's questions arise. Each
   supporting paragraph is followed, where it exists, by the strongest
   evidence against or the main alternative explanation. Unknowns are stated
   as unknowns.
4. **Kicker: close with the decision or the open question**, not a summary of
   what was already said and not a flourish.

Misuses to avoid: a nut graf that promises more than the body proves; one
that repeats the lede in abstract words; burying it below the first figure;
a report so formulaic that every issue has a "story" when nothing happened.

## Cover the bases: six story blocks

William Blundell, a former Journal page-one writer and writing coach,
organised material around six blocks (as described by Chip Scanlan at
Poynter). Use them as a reporting checklist for any exception or analysis,
and as the skeleton of the body. Skip a block only deliberately.

| Block | Question | Example, fictional online furniture shop (figures invented): conversion fell to 24% |
|---|---|---|
| **History** | When did it start; what led here? | first week below 28% since March; price list v7 on 28 Sep |
| **Scope** | How big; how many affected; how much money? | 412 design consultations a week; about €12,000 a month per point |
| **Reasons** | What forces drive it? | mix shift toward Meta leads; v7 delivery fee +€40 |
| **Impact** | Who gains or loses, and how much? | sofa orders most affected; delivery slots under-filled mid-October |
| **Countermoves** | Who is acting on it, and with what effect? | Meta spend capped since 2 Oct; sales calling back open consultations |
| **Future** | What happens if nothing changes, per the people and data involved? | October orders ~15% under plan on the current trend (forecast v3) |

The same blocks work for research: history (prior results), scope (how
many benchmarks, effect size), reasons (mechanism), impact (who should
change practice), countermoves (competing methods, failed replications),
future (open questions, what would falsify it).

## Sentence rules

- **Numbers carry unit, period and base.** "Revenue was €182,400 in
  September, up 9% from August" not "revenue grew strongly".
- **Attribute every claim.** Data: "according to the order service
  (retrieved 5 Oct)". People: name and role. Inference: say whose ("the
  analysis suggests"). An unattributed claim is the writer's opinion; label
  it or cut it.
- **Separate reporting from opinion.** Findings and recommendations live in
  separately labelled sections. A recommendation never appears as a finding.
- **Verbs proportional to evidence.** "rose", "fell", "was unchanged";
  causal verbs only as `statistical-claims` allows. No "skyrocketed",
  "plummeted", "game-changing", "massive", "unprecedented" unless the number
  and the full history justify it.
- **Concrete and short.** Subject, verb, number. Active voice. One idea per
  sentence; most sentences under about 25 words. Delete "very", "really",
  "significantly" (unless statistical, and then define it).
- **Names.** Full name and role at first mention, surname after; no
  courtesy titles (Mr., Ms., Dr. as an honorific). The Journal reportedly
  dropped courtesy titles in 2023. Occupational titles are kept ("analyst",
  "CFO"); that part is our own rule, not something the reports describe.
  In a small team's internal briefing, first names are acceptable if used
  consistently.
- **Company names** without legal suffixes (Inc., Corp., Oy, Ab) in running
  text unless the legal entity matters (also reportedly dropped by the
  Journal in 2023).
- **"Said" is the attribution verb.** Not "claimed", "admitted",
  "revealed", "slammed": those editorialise. Attribution goes after the fact
  it supports, not before a string of clauses.
- **Quotes for judgement and colour, not for facts.** A number belongs in
  your sentence with its source; a quote earns its place when it carries a
  view or a voice ("We lost three sofa orders to the delivery fee this week," the
  head of customer service said). Never paraphrase a person into more certainty
  than they expressed.
- **Absolute time.** "the week to 4 October", "since 28 September", not
  "last week" or "recently": briefings are read later and forwarded.
- **One precise hedge, not many vague ones.** "The data cannot yet separate
  price from mix" beats "this may possibly suggest".
- **Explain jargon once, in passing.** "ROAS, revenue per euro of ad spend,
  …"; then use the term.
- **No new facts while editing.** An edit may cut, reorder and sharpen. It
  may not introduce a number, cause or quote that is not in the evidence.

## Editing pass (classify → diagnose → revise → verify)

Run `references/editing-pass.md` on any draft: classify the piece (brief,
exception, analysis), check the nut graf exists by paragraph 4, flag every
unattributed claim and every number without unit/period/base, revise, then
verify that every number in the revision still matches the evidence.

## Good / bad

Bad:
> This week we saw some really interesting developments in conversion,
> which dropped significantly, probably due to the new pricing.

Good (illustrative; a fictional online furniture shop, figures invented):
> Consultation-to-order conversion fell to 24% in the week to 4 October, from
> 31% a week earlier, on 412 design consultations, according to the order
> service.
>
> The drop matters because October opens the pre-holiday buying season: at
> about 1,800 consultations a month and September's average order value of
> €690, each lost point of conversion is roughly €12,000 of monthly revenue.
> [nut graf]
>
> The fall coincided with price list v7, which raised the delivery fee by
> €40. But the share of consultations from Meta ads, which convert at half
> the rate of referrals, also rose from 18% to 29%; the data do not yet
> separate the two. [support, then counter-evidence]

## Checklist

- [ ] Form chosen: inverted pyramid (brief) or lede + nut graf (analysis).
- [ ] Nut graf present by paragraph 2–4; states what changed and why it matters now.
- [ ] Body covers history, scope, reasons, impact, countermoves, future (or skips one deliberately).
- [ ] Kicker is a decision or open question; attribution verb "said"; absolute dates.
- [ ] Every number has unit, period and comparison base.
- [ ] Every claim attributed; opinion and recommendation labelled.
- [ ] Strongest counter-evidence or alternative explanation included.
- [ ] No hype words; causal verbs match the design.
- [ ] No courtesy titles; no legal suffixes in running text.
- [ ] Editing introduced no new facts; numbers re-verified.
