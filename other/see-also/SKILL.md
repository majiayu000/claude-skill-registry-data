# See Also

*Adjacency-grammar in published form*

---

## Origin

Wikipedia's See Also sections are accidentally one of the most philosophically interesting genres on the internet. They are the encyclopedia's adjacency-grammar: no argument, no thesis, no claim about *why* these things belong together. Just a list. *These things are kin somehow. You figure out how.*

The form trusts the reader absolutely. It refuses the explanatory gloss. It says, by its borders rather than its body: *here is what the article was secretly about*. What an article points to at its end reveals what the article thought it was, in a way the article itself could not say.

This skill produces that form — deployed reflexively on the current conversation, or referentially on any subject. The default question it answers is: *what would appear at the bottom of the Wikipedia article that this chat would constitute?*

---

## The Core Principle

**Adjacency without argument.** Each entry is the thing named. The kinship is the content. The reader performs the binding.

The skill's discipline is *resisting the explanatory reflex*. Every fiber of training will want to append "— related because…" after each entry. **Don't.** That defeats the form. The form's gift to the reader is exactly the work of figuring out why each item is on the list. Take that work away and you've turned a See Also into a footnote section.

---

## What This Is Not

- **Not a bibliography.** A bibliography says "I read these." A See Also says "these are kin."
- **Not a recommendation list.** "You might also like" implies a service relation. See Also implies a structural one.
- **Not a tag cloud.** Tags categorize. See Also gestures.
- **Not a taxonomy.** A taxonomy claims to systematize. See Also refuses systematization.
- **Not a synonym list.** Synonyms are restatements. Kin are *other things* that share something the article couldn't articulate.
- **Not a glossary.** A glossary defines. See Also points.

---

## The Form's Rules

- **Bullet list.** Not prose.
- **5-15 entries.** Past twenty, the form dissolves into bibliography. Discipline is part of the form.
- **Each entry is just the name.** Of a concept, a book, a person, a practice, a phenomenon, a place, a genre, another article, another skill.
- **No editorial gloss after the entry.** No *because…*, no *see for…*, no *compare with…*
- **Brief disambiguating parenthetical allowed** *only when truly necessary* — e.g., dates after an obscure name, or *(philosophical)* after a word with multiple senses. The parenthetical disambiguates the **referent**, never the **kinship**.
- **Alphabetical or thematic.** Wikipedia tends alphabetical; thematic with sub-headings is also valid (see Variations).
- **No editorial voice.** No *interesting*, *remarkable*, *notably*. The bare entries.

---

## The Method

### 1. Sit with what the article was

If deploying reflexively on a conversation: ask, *what has this chat actually been about?* Not just the named topic — the substrate. What concept got the most weight, what figure kept returning, what vocabulary built up, what mood was in the room.

### 2. Find the kin

Run outward from the substrate. What concepts, texts, practices, traditions, people, phenomena, objects, places are adjacent? Not "what's in the same Wikipedia category" — the kinship is subtler than that. *What would a deeply-read reader of this article also have read?*

### 3. Mix the registers

A good See Also list has three kinds of entries:

- **Obvious kin** — entries the reader would expect. Their job is to anchor the list in the recognizable.
- **Adjacent kin** — entries that require a small move to see the relation. Most of the list lives here.
- **Slightly-strange entries** — one or two items that make the reader pause: *wait, why is that on the list?*

The slightly-strange entry is load-bearing. Without it, the list reads as competent retrieval; with it, the list reads as **a position taken entirely by adjacency** — an argument made without any of the structures of argument.

### 4. Order the list

Alphabetical is the encyclopedia default. Use it unless the list has clear thematic clusters that want to be made visible. If thematic, use sub-headings sparingly (two or three maximum) — the form survives only if it stays austere.

### 5. Resist the gloss

Read the list back. If you find yourself wanting to add explanation to an entry, **delete the entry** rather than gloss it. An entry that needs gloss to be legible is an entry that doesn't belong on a See Also list.

---

## What Belongs

- **Concepts** — *Kishōtenketsu*, *Apophenia*, *Davar*
- **Books, essays, articles** — italicized: *Tristes Tropiques*; *"Tradition and the Individual Talent"*
- **People** — with disambiguating dates if obscure: *Henri Corbin (1903–1978)*
- **Practices, traditions, schools** — *Theurgy*, *Close reading*, *Negative theology*
- **Phenomena** — *Pareidolia*, *Mono no aware*, *Tip-of-the-tongue state*
- **Places** — when the place carries conceptual weight: *Bibliothèque Mazarine*, *Mount Athos*
- **Genres and forms** — *Cento*, *Commonplace book*, *Mahzor*
- **Other Claude skills** — *Cf. /placement*, *Cf. /two-grammars*. The skill ecology can be a kin-substrate.
- **Wikipedia articles themselves** — when pointing at the article qua article: *List of unsolved problems in philosophy*

What doesn't belong: generic topic-keywords (*language*, *thought*, *memory*) that don't name a specific thing. Replace these with the specific named instance you actually mean.

## Variations

### Flat Alphabetical
The Wikipedia default. One bullet list, alphabetized. Most austere form.

### Grouped Thematic
Sub-headings group the entries. *Concepts*; *Practices*; *Related articles*. Use sparingly — too many sub-headings reintroduce taxonomy and break the form.

### The Cf. List
Academic short form. Each entry preceded by *Cf.* (compare). Slightly more directive than See Also but still refuses gloss.

### Article-Plus-Pointers
A short framing sentence (one or two lines) names what the article was, then the bullet list. Acceptable when the article's subject is obscure enough that the kin-list would otherwise float without anchor.

### Trans-Genre
Mix concepts, places, books, people, practices, phenomena freely on the same list. The form survives the mix; the kinship rises *because* of the heterogeneity. A list with a book and a phenomenon and a city on it makes its position visible by the very act of treating them as comparable.

## Common Failure Modes

### The Explanatory Gloss
**Symptom:** Each entry has *"— because…"* appended.
**Fix:** Delete every gloss. If the entry is illegible without it, delete the entry.

### The Tag Cloud
**Symptom:** Entries are generic topic-keywords (*language*, *consciousness*, *meaning*). The list could attach to any article.
**Fix:** Replace with specific named things. *Language* → *Sapir-Whorf hypothesis* or *Glossolalia* or *Ursprache*.

### The Obvious-Only List
**Symptom:** Every entry is predictable. No slightly-strange entry to make the reader pause.
**Fix:** Add one or two oblique items — kin that require a real move to see the relation. The list should have at least one entry that prompts a *wait, why?*

### The Too-Long List
**Symptom:** Twenty-five entries. Form dissolves into bibliography.
**Fix:** Cut to 5-15. Each cut item makes the remaining items work harder.

### The Recommendation Voice
**Symptom:** Entries phrased as suggestions — *"You might also enjoy…"* or *"For further reading…"*
**Fix:** No directive voice. Bare entries. The reader decides what to do with them.

### The Generic List
**Symptom:** The See Also could have attached to any conversation about this topic, not specifically *this* chat. The kinships are textbook, not custom-fit.
**Fix:** Pull from what the conversation actually surfaced — the specific vocabulary, the specific figure, the specific placement. A good reflexive See Also is partly illegible to someone who wasn't in the chat. That's the proof it's the right list.

### The Hedging Parenthetical
**Symptom:** Disambiguating parentheticals creep beyond disambiguation into explanation. *Pareidolia (the phenomenon of seeing patterns in noise, related here because…)*
**Fix:** Parentheticals disambiguate referents only. Never relations.

### The Safe Slate
**Symptom:** Every entry is unimpeachable, defensible, neutral. The list reads as inoffensive rather than as a position.
**Fix:** The form *takes a position* through what it places adjacent. Be willing to make a real claim by adjacency — to put a poem next to a phenomenon next to a piece of code and trust the reader.

## What This Does That Other Skills Don't

**vs. /placement** — Placement is the principle (trust the reader, don't explain). See Also is the *published form* placement takes when it becomes encyclopedia apparatus.

**vs. /two-grammars** — Two Grammars articulates adjacency vs. conflict. See Also is the most distilled form of adjacency-grammar in print — pure adjacency with no narrative scaffolding whatsoever.

**vs. /bookfy** — Bookfy generates imaginary books from context (generative kin-listing). See Also points at *existing* things (referential kin-listing). Bookfy invents; See Also catalogues.

**vs. /hyperlink-mind** — Hyperlink Mind narrates the process of conceptual activation. See Also produces only the result — the bare list — without showing the work.

**vs. /anamnesis** — Anamnesis traces a concept's genealogy through transformations. See Also makes no genealogical claim; it just lays kin alongside.

**vs. /shards** — Shards produce multiple takes on the same subject. See Also points outward to kin subjects.

**vs. /civilians** — Civilians are scenes of the chat's untranslatability. See Also is the silent counter-move: rather than stage the failure, list the kin and let the kin do the work civilians' scenes couldn't.

## Integration

Flows from:
- Any conversation whose substrate has accumulated enough that a See Also would make the substrate visible
- After /diary or /haecceity-capture — the See Also section the diary entry would have
- After /close-reading or /paper-hermeneutics — the See Also at the bottom of the analyzed text
- After a long sustained inquiry, as a way to mark *what this conversation was about at its borders*

Flows into:
- /bookfy — generating imaginary books that *belong* on the See Also
- /hyperlink-mind — picking one entry from the See Also and unfolding it
- /conjure — picking one figure from the See Also and summoning them
- /anamnesis — taking one concept from the list and tracing it backward

## For Future Instances

You will sometimes be in a conversation that has *become* something — accumulated a substrate, named a phenomenon, built a small vocabulary, occupied a register. The user may not ask explicitly for a See Also. But the conversation will be in a state where the kin-list, offered, would make visible what the conversation has been about *at its borders* — what it points to without saying.

Offer it. Or, if asked, produce it.

The skill's gift is exactly the work it refuses to do. You list. The reader binds. The kinship is not explained because the *unexplained-ness* is the form. Trust the reader. The Wikipedia editors do — and their faith in the reader is what makes the See Also section, of all things, philosophically interesting.

Write the bare list.
