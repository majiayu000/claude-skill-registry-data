# Trade Vocabulary

The reader of a report is the operator. To them, "your beverage attach rate" is plainer and sharper than "how often guests order a drink." Naming a problem or lever in the trade's own **metric** signals you understand their business — the way a mechanic using the right part name signals they know cars.

Assume plain, hook-first, short writing is already your default. This skill governs *which word* you reach for when the trade has a precise one, on top of that.

**Core rule: when the trade has a precise term for a metric or lever in this finding, use it in place of the generic phrase — but only when it is genuinely sharper, and never to claim something the evidence doesn't show.**

---

## This is about their metrics, not their slang

The value is in the **KPI/lever names** operators actually manage by:

| Generic (weaker) | Operator term (sharper) |
|---|---|
| how often guests order a drink | beverage **attach rate** |
| customers who don't come back | your **rebook rate** / losing **regulars** |
| a repeat service call for the same problem | a **callback** |
| how many guests you serve | **covers** (guests) / **turns** (reseats per table) |
| whether the problem is service or food | **FOH** vs **BOH** |
| average spend per guest | **PPA** / **check average** |

Reach for the *metric*. Do **not** sprinkle in-group slang ("in the weeds," "slammed") for flavor — it reads as trying too hard, and a stranger using a trade's social slang lands as pandering, not competence. Naming the *number they manage by* is the opposite: it's respect.

---

## The guardrail (this is the whole skill)

**Use the term for what the evidence shows. Never to assert a cause the evidence doesn't.**

The failure mode is real. Given a review that says *"our drinks come watered down"*:

**Works** — names the lever, stays in evidence:
> "The lever is your beverage attach rate. No five-star review names a drink; the only mention is 'watered down.'"

**Fails** — the term drags in an invented operator-side cause:
> "The well is slow and the pour quality reads as watered down."

The review says the drink was weak. It does **not** say the well (the bar's speed rail) is slow — that's a cause you made up because the vocabulary was available. A trade term used to smuggle in an unsupported claim is worse than plain English: it's confident *and* wrong, and an operator catches it instantly.

---

## How to apply — three tests per term

Before swapping in a trade term, all three must be yes:

1. **Sharper?** Is the term genuinely tighter than the plain phrase — or already in the finding data? If the plain words are just as clear, or the term's already there, leave it. (Swapping "LTO" in where "LTO" already sits adds nothing.)
2. **Fits?** Does this exact business use this term? "Covers/turns" is full-service; a coffee shop has neither. Scope to the sub-sector.
3. **Inside the evidence?** Does the term describe only what a review, benchmark, or count actually shows — no invented cause, no assumed mechanism?

One good swap that names the operator's real lever beats five slang drop-ins.

## When NOT to use

- **Plain-language / no-jargon deliverables.** A self-serve product for a buyer who asked for zero consultant-speak should *strip* trade terms. Do not apply this skill there.
- **A different audience than the operator.** Broker, lender and enterprise briefs speak valuation/deal language, not the operator's KPIs. A term only helps when the *reader* manages by it. If you're unsure the reader is the operator, write plain.

## Common mistakes

- **Slang for flavor.** "Your kitchen's in the weeds" — cut it. Name the metric (ticket times), not the mood.
- **Cause-smuggling.** The guardrail failure above. The term must not carry a claim the evidence doesn't.
- **Forcing a term that half-fits.** If you're reaching, the plain phrase is safer.
- **Term already in the data.** No credit for re-stating "LTO" — the swap has to add precision.
