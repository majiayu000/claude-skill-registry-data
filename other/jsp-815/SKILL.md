# JSP 815: Defence Safety Management System (Framework and Direction and Guidance)

## When to use
**Prerequisites:** none: plain Markdown. Familiarity with basic risk vocabulary (hazard, risk, ALARP, assurance) helps; the glossary defines the Defence-specific terms.

Use this skill for questions about how the UK Ministry of Defence manages safety: which JSP answers which need, how a Defence Area structures its safety management arrangements, what the 12 Elements and their Expectations require, when a safety case is needed, how exemption certificates work, how competence and delegation are controlled, how equipment and infrastructure safety is organised, and how assurance and audit are conducted. It is a single-source pack: everything in it derives from JSP 815 V1.3 (2026-09-01) and its Annexes A to F and H.

## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### The Defence SMS in one view
JSP 815 sets the Defence Safety Management System (SMS): a goal-based, non-prescriptive framework under which each Defence Area builds its own safety management arrangements within a common structure. Accountability is devolved; the Department sets Expectations, and Defence Areas conform to them through their own governance and operational context. The SMS is aligned to ISO 45001 but carries additional and different requirements to meet Defence needs, so ISO alignment is a claim the Framework makes, not an identity. The Defence Safety Function, led by the Director of Defence Safety & Support (Dir-DSS), owns the SMS, the Safety Operating Model, and second-line assurance against it.

### Plan-Do-Check-Act basis
The SMS is organised on the four-stage Plan-Do-Check-Act cycle. Plan covers policy, governance, and planning; Do implements the arrangements and controls; Check measures performance and investigates failures; Act reviews and improves. The 12 Elements distribute across this cycle, and continual improvement is the loop that closes it.

### Element / Expectation / Performance Statement hierarchy
The structure has three levels. **Elements** (12) divide Defence Area activity into safety topics. Each Element carries numbered **Expectations** (72 in total, E1.1 to E12.5) describing activities expected to be in place in a Defence Area's arrangements. Each Expectation is articulated by **Performance Statements** that show how compliance and continual improvement are demonstrated, with example performance across assurance levels from unsatisfactory to substantial. Cite Expectation numbers; treat Performance Statements as themes this pack summarises rather than text it reproduces.

### The 12 Elements
- **E1** Leadership, Governance and Culture (6 Expectations): tone from the top, accountabilities, leadership visibility, resilient safety management.
- **E2** Organisation and Dependencies (8): safety management arrangements, roles, resources, consultation, changes, dependencies and interfaces. Annex A covers SEMS applicability.
- **E3** Legislation, Policy, Regulations and Guidance (5): compliance, Defence Area policy, communicating requirements, exemptions. Annex B covers the exemption certificate process.
- **E4** Risk Assessments and Safety Cases (7): hazard identification, risk management and elevation, communication, change tracking, safety cases.
- **E5** Supervision, Contracting and Control Activities (7): delegation of authority, risk elevation, letters of delegation, ceasing activities, safe systems of work. Annexes C and D cover duty of care on deployments and Head of Establishment responsibilities.
- **E6** Personnel Competence, Resources and Training (5): resources, delegation, people development, training, competency assessment. Annex E covers safety aspects of letters of delegation.
- **E7** Equipment Design, Manufacture and Maintenance (8): lifecycle risks, compliance, maintenance, supply chain, lessons, integration.
- **E8** Infrastructure Design, Build and Maintenance (7): the infrastructure counterpart to E7.
- **E9** Performance, Management Information and Reporting (4): monitoring, review, management information, leadership decisions.
- **E10** Accident / Incident Management and Emergency Response (5): reporting, recording, investigation, actions and learning, emergency and business continuity plans.
- **E11** Communications and Stakeholder Engagement (5): stakeholders, working relations, accessing safety information, raising concerns.
- **E12** Assurance (5): first, second, and third Lines of Defence assurance, annual self-assessment, leadership review, corrective action. Annex F maps Elements to ISO 45001 clause numbers; Annex H (V1.2) is the audit manual.

### Assurance stages
Assurance levels are sequential and cumulative: unsatisfactory, limited, moderate, substantial. A Defence Area can only claim substantial assurance once moderate has been achieved. The assessment criteria are aligned with the Government Internal Audit Agency and ask three questions of key controls: are they embedded, do they help manage key risks, and do they operate as intended. Element 12 operationalises this through the three-Lines-of-Defence model: first-line assurance by those delivering the activity, second-line function assurance, and third-line independent audit per the Annex H manual.

### Safety case trigger (E4.7)
A safety case is a structured argument, supported by evidence, that justifies a system as safe for a given application in a defined operating environment. The trigger: Defence work-related activity conducted on, or forming part of, a system where a single risk assessment of the activity or workplace is not sufficient to demonstrate adequate risk control, because of complexity, novelty, scale, or the risk profile. Simple, routine activity kept ALARP and tolerable by risk assessments and standard operating procedures does not need one. Scope is proportionate to risk and complexity, and equipment safety cases are treated in Element 7.

### Exemption route (E3.5, Annex B)
Where compliance with safety legislation cannot be achieved, an exemption certificate from the Secretary of State for Defence is the route. The process is a last resort, used only after other avenues are exhausted. A sponsor builds an Exemption Case Summary, gets it endorsed, forwards it with a draft certificate for approval, and then provides a copy to the regulator, records the certificate, and manages its validity and renewal while control measures limit residual risk. Where no certificate is in place, the activity must conform.
