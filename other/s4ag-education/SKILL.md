# Education

You have knowledge and a living system worth sharing. Farm-based education turns that into a purposeful programme — for school groups, workshop learners, course participants, or an online audience — while creating a legitimate income stream and community connection. The challenge is designing an experience that is honest about farming, age-appropriate for the audience, and structured enough to deliver without consuming the farm. Every programme should leave learners with a changed relationship to soil, food, and where it comes from.

**How this skill works:** Each sub-tool pauses at a **Checkpoint** to confirm the assumptions it is about to build on before producing output. A programme designed for the wrong audience, the wrong season, or the wrong farm context will fail in practice — confirm the checkpoint before acting. Each sub-tool ends with **Next steps** — the skills worth running once you have acted on this one.

## Expert Lineage

*The thinkers whose frameworks underpin this skill — and what they specifically discovered that changes how you farm.*

**Growing Home (Chicago) — Workforce Development as Education**
Growing Home demonstrated that farm-based education works most powerfully when it is connected to tangible livelihood outcomes. Their urban agriculture workforce development programme showed that learners who are employed on the farm while learning outperform classroom-only cohorts in retention and practical competence. Implication: the most effective farm education combines earning with learning — even a small payment or produce share transforms participant commitment.

**Groundswell (UK) — Farmer-to-Farmer Education**
Groundswell, the no-till and regenerative farming conference, established that farmer-to-farmer knowledge transfer is qualitatively different from expert-to-farmer transfer. Farmers trust evidence presented by other practitioners over academic or extension sources. Their model — demonstration farms, peer discussion, field walks — consistently produces faster adoption of new practice than conventional extension. Implication: your lived experience as a farmer is your primary educational asset. Design programmes around what you have done and observed, not around what you have read.

**Elaine Ingham — Soil Biology as the Core Learning Outcome**
Ingham's work establishes that a single hands-on experience — pulling up a soil core, counting earthworms, doing a jar test — produces a shift in how learners relate to soil that no amount of lecture achieves. Her outreach work shows that children who discover that soil is alive change their relationship with food production. Implication: every farm education programme, regardless of age or focus, should include a hands-on soil biology moment as a non-negotiable centrepiece.

**Robin Wall Kimmerer — Reciprocity as Pedagogy**
Kimmerer's work on traditional ecological knowledge demonstrates that the most durable learning happens when the learner gives something back to the system they are studying. Her concept of the Honorable Harvest — ask permission, take only what you need, give thanks, give back — provides a pedagogical framework that works for both children and adults. Implication: design the learning experience so participants contribute something to the farm, even something small — planting a seed, spreading compost, weeding a bed — and the experience becomes reciprocal rather than extractive.

**Jean-Martin Fortier — Farm Systems as Teachable Units**
Fortier's documented, metric-driven approach to market gardening makes his production system uniquely teachable — everything is measured, sequenced, and justified. His contribution to education is demonstrating that a coherent, replicable farm system can be taught in a short intensive. Implication: the clearest educational programmes are built around a farm that has a distinct, legible system — not a farm that does many things approximately.

**Weston A. Price Foundation — Food Quality as the Entry Point**
Price's research established that nutritional density in food is directly connected to how it is grown. The WAPF's educational work demonstrates that the most effective way to shift public behaviour around food is through taste and quality experience, not through facts. Implication: the most powerful moment in a farm education programme is often the tasting — the tomato still warm from the sun, the egg from the morning's collection. Design for sensory experience first, explanation second.

## School Visits

*Designs a school farm visit that is safe, curriculum-connected, and worth repeating.*

A good school visit achieves one thing: it makes children feel that food comes from somewhere alive, tended by people who know it. Everything else — the curriculum tick-boxes, the safety forms, the activity schedule — is scaffolding around that central experience. Design from the central experience outward, not from the admin inward.

**Age-group calibration:**

| Age | Attention span | Best activities | What they remember |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3–5 (Early years) | 10–15 min per activity | Sensory — soil, worms, smelling herbs, collecting eggs | The feeling and the animal contact |
| 5–7 (KS1 / Grade 1–2) | 15–20 min | Planting seeds, simple harvest, animal feeding | The thing they planted or held |
| 7–11 (KS2 / Grade 2–5) | 20–30 min | Soil investigation, growing cycles, compost making | The soil science moment, the story of the farm |
| 11–14 (KS3 / Grade 6–8) | 30–45 min | Food web, enterprise context, sustainability questions | The systems thinking — why this farm works differently |
| 14–18 (KS4-5 / Grade 9–12) | Open-ended | Full farm walk, data collection, enterprise economics | The honesty about difficulty and tradeoffs |

**Visit structure (2-hour visit):**

1. **Arrival and orientation (15 min)** — where they are, what happens here, safety rules in plain language. One compelling image or fact: "This soil has more living organisms in a teaspoon than there are people on earth."

2. **Soil moment (20–30 min)** — every visit, every age. Give each child a jar of water and some soil. Watch it settle. Pull a soil core. Count earthworms in a sample. The biology is visible and it works every time.

3. **Main activity (45–60 min)** — age-calibrated from the table above. Plant something, harvest something, make something. If nothing is ready to harvest, this is a failure point — plan the visit calendar around what the farm has ready.

4. **Animal contact or crop tasting (15 min)** — where available. A warm tomato or a strawberry from the plant changes the visit irreversibly. Animal contact at appropriate biosecurity distance is the second most memorable moment.

5. **Debrief and questions (10–15 min)** — what surprised them? What do they want to know? The question period tells you what landed and what to adjust.

**Curriculum connections (UK National Curriculum / US NGSS):**

| Topic | Key stage / grade | Farm hook |
|---|---|---|
| Plants and life cycles | KS1–2 / G1–4 | Seed to harvest on the farm |
| Soil and ecosystems | KS2–3 / G3–6 | Soil food web, earthworm count |
| Nutrition and food | KS2–3 / G3–6 | Where food comes from; nutritional density |
| Environmental science | KS3–4 / G6–9 | Carbon, biodiversity, water cycle on farm |
| Business and enterprise | KS4–5 / G9–12 | Farm enterprise, direct marketing, margin |
| Geography | KS3–4 / G6–9 | Land use, food systems, local vs global |

**Safety and compliance (minimum requirements):**
- Risk assessment in writing, reviewed before each visit
- DBS/background check if children are on-farm without teachers
- Public liability insurance that explicitly covers educational visits
- Teacher:child ratio respected — you are not responsible for supervision ratios, the school is
- No-go zones marked clearly before the group arrives
- Hand-washing station available after soil and animal contact

**Soil health note:** The earthworm count and jar test are not just activities — they are the most effective soil communication tools available. A child who finds twelve earthworms in a spade of soil has learned more about biological agriculture in three minutes than any explanation achieves. Include it in every visit regardless of the stated topic.

**Checkpoint — confirm before finalising:**
- What is the age group and school year, and how many children?
- What is the visit date and what crops or animals will genuinely be active and ready for interaction at that time?
- Does your insurance explicitly cover educational visits and is your risk assessment current?

Producing an activity plan for the wrong season or without checking insurance cover creates a visit that either disappoints (nothing to show or harvest) or carries unacceptable liability risk.

## Workshops

*Plans and runs a practical farm workshop — one day to multi-day — that delivers genuine skill transfer.*

A workshop is not a lecture with activities. It is a structured experience in which participants leave having done something they could not do before. The farm's job is to set up the conditions; the participant does the learning. Design the doing sequence first, then the explanation that supports it.

**Workshop format decision:**

| Format | Duration | Best for | Revenue model |
|---|---|---|---|
| Taster | Half day (3–4h) | First-time visitors, single topic | Low ticket price, high throughput |
| Day course | Full day (6–7h) | One complete skill unit | Mid-range ticket; most common format |
| Weekend intensive | 2 days | Complex skill (cheesemaking, fermentation, grafting) | Highest ticket price; lowest frequency |
| Multi-day residential | 3–5 days | Full system overview (e.g., no-dig market garden) | Premium price; accommodation required |
| Evening session | 2h | Community-facing, low barrier | Low price or free; builds email list |

**Workshop design sequence:**

1. **Define the single outcome.** What can the participant do at the end that they could not do at the start? Be specific: "make and apply a batch of aerated compost tea" not "understand compost."

2. **Map the skill steps.** What sequence of actions produces the outcome? List every step in order. This becomes the workshop timetable.

3. **Design the doing blocks.** Each step should be demonstrated, then done. Allow 50–60% of time for participants to do the thing, not watch it.

4. **Identify what participants take home.** A physical output — a seedling, a jar of kombucha, a bag of compost, a drafted plan — anchors the learning in a tangible way and is worth paying for.

5. **Plan the soil biology moment.** Even in a workshop on grafting or cheesemaking, there is a soil connection. Build it in — five minutes on how this practice connects to living soil systems, illustrated by something they can touch.

**Pricing framework:**

```
Workshop revenue = [ticket price] × [participants]
Ticket price = (all direct costs / participants) + your time cost + desired margin

Direct costs: materials consumed, any brought-in co-facilitator, catering if provided
Your time: preparation + delivery + follow-up (often 2–3× the workshop day itself)
Comfortable participant number: 8–14 for skill-based workshops (above 14, doing time drops)
Minimum viable number: set at 4–6 and cancel or reschedule if below this
```

**Common workshop topics by farm type:**

| Farm type | Strongest workshop topics |
|---|---|
| Market garden | No-dig growing, soil biology basics, succession planting, compost |
| Livestock | Rotational grazing, cheesemaking, natural health management |
| Agroforestry / food forest | Pruning, grafting, food forest design, foraging |
| Mixed / regenerative | Soil food web, regenerative principles, farm planning |
| Specialist crops | Herb growing and drying, seed saving, fermentation |

**What goes wrong:**

| Problem | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Low bookings | Wrong audience, wrong platform, wrong time of year | Survey existing contacts first; run one taster before investing in a full programme |
| Participants don't learn the skill | Too much talking, not enough doing | Cut explanation by 30%; extend doing time |
| Over-subscribed, quality drops | No participant cap | Set a hard cap; charge more rather than add people |
| Facilitator exhaustion | Too many workshops in a row | Maximum one per month to start; assess demand before scaling |
| Refund requests | Expectations unclear at booking | Write a detailed booking description; specify what participants make and take |

**Soil health integration:** Build the soil biology connection into every workshop, whatever the topic. In a grafting workshop: explain that mycorrhizal inoculation at potting stage transforms establishment. In a cheesemaking workshop: draw the connection between the microbial biodiversity in raw milk and the biodiversity in healthy soil — the same principles govern fermentation in both systems. This is not a lecture — it is a five-minute contextualisation that makes the whole day more meaningful.

**Checkpoint — confirm before finalising:**
- What is the single skill outcome — what can participants do at the end that they could not before?
- How many participants, and is the farm physically set up to run that number safely and effectively?
- What is the participant take-home — what do they leave with in their hands?

A workshop without a clear single outcome produces a pleasant day that participants cannot describe or recommend. Confirm the outcome before building anything else.

## Learning Farm

*Establishes the farm as a structured, ongoing educational institution — with programmes, partnerships, and a sustainable model.*

A learning farm is not a farm that sometimes runs workshops. It is a farm in which education is a designed, integrated enterprise with its own identity, calendar, partnerships, and revenue model. The distinction matters because the investment required — in infrastructure, time, and coordination — only pays if education is treated as a proper enterprise, not an add-on.

**Programme architecture:**

A sustainable learning farm typically offers across three levels:

| Level | Format | Audience | Revenue |
|---|---|---|---|
| Access | School visits, open days, community events | Schools, general public | Per-group or low ticket |
| Skill | Day workshops, evening sessions | Adults seeking specific skills | Mid-range ticket |
| Depth | Apprenticeships, internships, residencies | Serious learners, career changers | High ticket or labour exchange |

Not every farm runs all three. Start with access (lower commitment, builds audience), develop skill (validates topics and demand), add depth only when the farm has the operational capacity to support extended learners.

**Apprenticeship and internship design:**

The depth level — apprenticeships and residencies — is the most valuable educationally but carries the most risk if poorly designed. Poor apprenticeships harm both parties.

Requirements for a functioning apprenticeship:
- A written agreement specifying: duration, hours, what the apprentice learns, what work they contribute, accommodation and food arrangements, who the supervisor is
- A clear learning curriculum — what skills, in what sequence, the apprentice will develop across the programme
- Regular formal review points — monthly is minimum; weekly is better
- An honest conversation at the start about what the farm needs vs. what the apprentice needs, and how those two are balanced
- Legal compliance check — apprenticeship arrangements may have employment law implications depending on jurisdiction

**Working without disconnecting the farm:**

The most common failure of learning farm programmes is that the farmer burns out hosting learners while running the production operation. The structural fix is to separate the educational offer from the production decision-making — participants learn by doing tasks the farm genuinely needs done, but the farmer is not the only person delivering explanation and context.

Solutions:
- Develop one or two senior apprentices who take on co-facilitation after their first season
- Create a curriculum document so the farmer is not re-explaining everything from scratch to every cohort
- Batch education delivery — all explanation and teaching in one morning block, production in the afternoon

**Partnership development:**

Learning farms that work long-term are embedded in a network of institutions:

| Partner type | What they offer | What you offer |
|---|---|---|
| Schools | Reliable group bookings, curriculum alignment | Living classroom, experiential learning |
| Agricultural colleges | Student placement, credentialing, funding access | Real-world application of college theory |
| Health and social services | Referral of participants in green social prescribing | Therapeutic outdoor environment, structured activity |
| Environmental NGOs | Funding, volunteer networks, public audience | Demonstration site, educational content |
| Food businesses | Funding, audience, legitimacy | Provenance story, engagement opportunity |

**Green social prescribing note:** Green social prescribing — GPs and social workers referring patients to nature-based activities as a health intervention — is a growing funding stream for learning farms in the UK and increasingly elsewhere. If you are developing a learning farm programme, explore this route early; it provides both funding and a steady participant stream. Requires some additional safeguarding and support capacity, but is manageable for most farms.

**Soil health as the central educational thread:**

Every learning farm programme should have soil biology as an explicit thread that runs through every session, every enterprise. The Ingham food web framework provides the curriculum: in week one, learners do a worm count and a jar test. By month three, every input decision they make is framed through the question "does this feed or kill the biology?" This is the most lasting thing a learning farm can teach — and it changes how graduates farm for the rest of their lives.

**Checkpoint — confirm before finalising:**
- Does the farm have reliable, year-round programme content — not just seasonal peaks? A learning farm that is active only in summer is not a sustainable enterprise.
- Is there sufficient physical infrastructure to host learners without compromising production — toilet facilities, workspace, somewhere to eat, basic accommodation if residential?
- Who will deliver the programme when you cannot — is there a co-facilitator or senior apprentice who can run sessions independently?

Launching a learning farm programme without year-round content and without a co-delivery plan creates a single point of failure that collapses the programme the first time you are ill, busy, or simply need time off.

## Online Education

*Translates farm knowledge into online content — courses, video, or membership — that reaches beyond the physical farm.*

Online education is not a lesser version of in-person learning. It is a different format that does different things: it reaches people who cannot come to the farm, it builds an audience before they arrive, and it creates income that does not require your physical presence. The challenge is that the qualities that make farm education compelling — sensory experience, place, unpredictability — are precisely what a screen cannot deliver. The skill is knowing what to translate and what to leave to the farm.

**What works online vs. what requires physical presence:**

| Works well online | Requires physical presence |
|---|---|
| Systems thinking — how the farm works | Hands-on skill development (planting, pruning, grafting) |
| Planning methodologies — rotation, scheduling | Animal handling |
| Identification knowledge — pests, plants, fungi | Soil sensory assessment |
| The economics of farming decisions | The feel of a healthy vs. compacted soil |
| Case study walkthroughs — decision by decision | Community and peer learning |
| Recorded farm walks — the narrated tour | Crisis decision-making in real conditions |

**Platform selection:**

| Platform type | Best for | Revenue model | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Course platform | Structured, self-paced courses | One-time purchase or subscription | Teachable, Thinkific, Kajabi |
| Video platform | Free content that builds audience | Advertising (low) or channel membership | YouTube |
| Membership platform | Ongoing access to content library + community | Monthly/annual membership | Substack, Patreon, Circle |
| Email newsletter | Building and sustaining an audience | Free; converts to paid offers | Substack, ConvertKit |
| Your own website | Full control, no platform dependency | Any model | Wordpress + WooCommerce |

**Recommended sequence for a new online educator:**

1. **Build the audience first.** Start with free content — a newsletter, a YouTube channel, a social media presence — before attempting to sell anything. Trying to sell to an audience of fifty people fails. An audience of two thousand converts reliably.

2. **Identify your one most-asked question.** What do people ask you about the most? At markets, at visits, in emails? This is your first product. Package it as a short, specific course or guide — not a comprehensive overview.

3. **Start with email.** Email subscribers convert to buyers at ten times the rate of social media followers. Every piece of free content should have a mechanism to collect an email address.

4. **Record a live session before building a studio.** Your first online course does not need professional production. Record a workshop you are already running. The content matters more than the production quality in every audience segment that a farm educator reaches.

5. **Price confidently.** Farmers systematically underprice online content. A course that replaces one day of workshop attendance should be priced at 50–70% of the workshop ticket price — the learner saves on travel and can revisit the content.

**Content formats ranked by production difficulty:**

| Format | Production difficulty | Audience size required | Revenue potential |
|---|---|---|---|
| Email newsletter | Low | Any size | Low direct; high conversion |
| Written course (PDF / web) | Low | 500+ | Medium |
| Recorded video course | Medium | 1,000+ | High |
| Live webinar | Medium | 500+ | Medium |
| Membership community | High (ongoing) | 2,000+ | Highest (recurring) |
| Podcast | Medium | 5,000+ for revenue | Audience building only |

**Soil biology online:** Soil biology is among the most shareable topics in farming — the worm count, the jar test, the microscope footage of a teaspoon of healthy soil — these images work powerfully online. Elaine Ingham's materials demonstrate this: video footage of the soil food web in motion is among the most viewed agricultural content on any platform. If you are building online content, make soil biology a recurring thread.

**Checkpoint — confirm before finalising:**
- Do you have an existing audience — email list, social following, or workshop alumni — large enough to generate early sales without paid advertising?
- What is the specific single question your first course will answer? Not "farming" or "regenerative growing" — one precise question with a practical answer.
- What is your production setup — camera, microphone, space to record — and is it realistic for what you are committing to?

Launching an online course to an audience of fewer than three hundred people with no marketing budget produces revenue that does not justify the production time. Confirm your audience before building.
