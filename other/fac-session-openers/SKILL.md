# Icebreakers and Session Openers

## When To Use
You need an opener that does not make people cringe. The plan is written, the first content block is known, and the slot before it still says "icebreaker" with nothing under it. This answers one question: what do people do in the first minutes that is already the work?

## When Not To Use
Not for the activities in any other block: Group Activity Bank covers those. Not for how the room works together or what is recorded: that is Session Agreements and Consent, which follows the opener. And never during the session: this is prepared beforehand, and you pick in the room.

## Inputs
- The first content block: its topic, its aim and what it needs from people.
- The group: roles, how many, whether they know each other, whether they chose to come.
- The room: in person or on your video platform, the time you can give, and anything from a pre-session survey.

If you have none of this, I start from the session topic and the group's roles and mark the output as a first draft.

## Approach
This pack's position, drawn from what participants complain about: an opener is the first exercise, not a separate game. Its question is about the topic or people's own work, and its answers are used in the block that follows. The formats are low-exposure ones: Impromptu Networking and 1-2-4-All from Liberating Structures, on its originators' site (shared under a Creative Commons licence), and a plain check-in round. Those pages describe steps, not proof that anything works. The failure this prevents: "share a fun fact about yourself" put to people who met moments ago, some of them with their director in the room.

## Workflow
1. Ask up to three questions: what the first content block must achieve, who is in the room and whether they chose to be there, and how much time and which setting (in person or virtual) you have.
2. Write one question per opener that belongs to the first block: the challenge people bring, a case from their work, what they want to be able to do. If the answers are not needed afterwards, the question is wrong, however charming.
3. Build three openers in three formats. Impromptu Networking: a moment of silent reflection, then short paired conversations over a few rounds with a new partner each time, then a brief share. 1-2-4-All: alone, pairs, fours, whole group on one question. Check-in round: one short work answer each, "pass" allowed, and too long for a large group, so say so.
4. For each, state the steps, `[minutes]` for you to set, the virtual variant (breakout pairs, a written round in chat), and how to take part without personal disclosure: a work answer is always enough and passing is fine.
5. Write the line that says how the answers feed the first block: what you collect, where it goes, which sentence links the two.
6. Name the one to drop if the room is senior, tired or sent by their boss, and why. Usually the one with the most movement or the most plenary talking goes first.
7. Hand over the check a person runs: of each opener you ask "would I be comfortable answering this in front of my own manager?" If not, it goes.

## Done When
- Three openers, three formats, each with a question about the topic or the work.
- Each one says how its answers are used in the first content block.
- Each has a way to take part without personal disclosure and a virtual variant.
- The one to drop is named with its reason.
- The trainer has run the manager check on all three and cut any that fail.

## Quality Bar
- No fun facts, no childhood stories, no "two truths", no props that need explaining.
- Every time is a bracketed placeholder; you set the real one for this group.
- Energisers are not a separate thing here: if the room needs moving, the next exercise does it.
- No opener asks for personal disclosure or sorts people into types, and you choose which one this room can take.
