# Outline

Commit to structure before prose. Prose written against no structure defaults to
chronology and monotony; every outline pattern below exists to force choices the
default avoids.

Pick the artifact by form:

- **Essay / article / newsletter** → a **spine contract** (spine, prerequisites,
  ordered blocks).
- **Novel / story / screenplay** → a **story engine**, then a **beat sheet**, then
  a **scene list**.
- **Script / episode** → a story engine plus act breaks and scene beats.

Mix freely for hybrids (memoir, narrative essay): the engine plus a block order.

## Shared loop

1. **Read everything first** — the fragment pile, the bible, the brief — in full,
   before proposing anything. An outline built on a partial read inherits the gaps.
2. **Establish the starting point.** What does the reader or audience bring?
   Concepts they already hold are **prerequisites**; everything else must be
   grounded inside the piece before a later block leans on it. This lever — how
   much you demand up front — decides who the piece is for.
3. **Draft the artifact** (below), as 2–3 candidates at the fork points, and let
   the user pick or hybridize. Structure is where disagreement is cheap; it gets
   expensive one page later.
4. **Pressure-test against the reader or audience.** Walk the artifact and at each
   unit ask: what does the reader need next? What question does this scene open?
   Every question the piece must answer becomes a block or scene; every question
   it won't answer, cut or postpone on purpose.
5. **Return the artifact as a contract** the draft stage can execute: one
   controlling sentence, the list of units, each unit's job, and the open gaps the
   material cannot yet fill. Name gaps instead of papering over them.

## Spine contract (essays and arguments)

- **Spine**: one sentence — the claim, not the topic. "Pricing pages fail because
  they answer questions buyers aren't asking yet" is a spine; "it's about pricing
  pages" is a topic. If you cannot write it in one sentence without "and", there is
  no spine yet: more capture, or name the missing claim.
- **Prerequisites**: the concepts the reader walks in with. Fixed up front.
- **Ordered blocks**, each with the one job it does for the reader that the
  previous block did not. Merge blocks with the same job; cut blocks with none.
- **The ending**: the last block must land the spine — a payoff, not a summary.

For narrative and literary essays, a conflict-free shape is legitimate:
**kishōtenketsu** (four movements: development, twist, reconciliation — the turn
carries the piece without an argument). Reach for it when the user's material is
associative rather than contentious; see
[references/narrative-architecture.md](references/narrative-architecture.md).

## Story engine (fiction and narrative nonfiction)

Settle these before any beat sheet, and write them down — they are the tests every
later scene must pass:

- **Want** — what the protagonist visibly pursues. Stated or not, the reader must
  be able to name it by the end of the first act.
- **Need** — what would actually fix their life, which they may not know. The
  distance between want and need is the character arc.
- **Lie / wound** — the false belief the story breaks.
- **Obstacle** — the force with a plausible chance of winning. No plausible
  opposition, no story.
- **Stakes ladder** — what each failure costs, and how the next stake escalates.
  Flat stakes are the most common dead-novel symptom; escalate by personal cost,
  not bigger explosions.
- **Dramatic question** — "will she…?" Answerable only at the climax.
- **Exit state** — how the protagonist ends up changed; the ending you are
  promising.

## Beat sheet, then scene list

The beat sheet is the promise order (act turns, midpoint, low point, climax).
The scene list is the promise kept scene by scene. For each scene:

> POV · goal (concrete this scene) · conflict · **turn** · exit state

The **turn** is the only non-negotiable field: something must be different at the
end of every scene — a door closed, a belief cracked, a fact learned. A scene
with no turn is summary wearing a scene's clothes; mark it `summary` and fold it
into a neighboring scene.

Connect scenes with **therefore / but** — never _and then_: "she escapes, _but_
the pilot is dead", "he confesses, _therefore_ she must choose". Two consecutive
_and then_ scenes are one scene, or one of them is dead weight.

Run **try-fail cycles**: each attempt ends _yes-but_ (succeeded, at a cost) or
_no-and_ (failed, and worse). A success that just works is a skipped scene.

## Series and long works

- **Promise-progress-payoff**: every promise the opening makes gets progress
  installments and a payoff; keep the ledger of promises made so none is dropped.
- **Escalation ladder**: each installment's problem outranks the last's by kind,
  not just size.
- **Mystery boxes**: each unanswered question is a ledger row with the book it
  must pay off in — never an unlisted promise.
- Hand established facts and character data to `writing-bible` as you go; the
  outline is promises, the bible is canon.

## Choosing a shape

Fit the pattern to the material, not the material to the pattern. Deciding
between three-act, kishōtenketsu, MICE quotient, or an episodic structure is a
real fork — see
[references/narrative-architecture.md](references/narrative-architecture.md) for
the patterns, what each buys, and how to nest subplots and POV threads. Offer the
choice to the user when the material could genuinely support two shapes.
