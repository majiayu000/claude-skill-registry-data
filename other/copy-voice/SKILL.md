# Copy Voice

Every word we write is selling something — a finding, a mailer, a website headline. The same principles apply to all of it.

The core rule: **the point of the first sentence is to get someone to read the second sentence.** Every sentence after that has the same job.

**This applies to charts too, not just prose.** A chart that needs a legend decoded before it means anything fails the same test as a jargon-heavy sentence: it makes the reader do work instead of pulling them forward. Every bar needs an axis or a comparison point, and a number that is already one clean sentence doesn't need a chart (see `ascii-charts`, "The 2-second test").

---

## The slippery slope

Good copy creates momentum. Each sentence pulls the reader to the next. The moment they have to re-read, or look up a word, or parse a long clause — they stop. And they don't come back.

Write so the reader melts into it.

---

## The rules

### Sentence length
Max 20 words. If a sentence runs long, split it. One idea per sentence.

### Paragraph length
Max 4 sentences. When you finish a thought, stop. White space is not wasted space.

### Word choice
Use words with one or two syllables when you can. If a simpler word exists, use it.

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| leverage | use |
| utilize | use |
| facilitate | help |
| optimize | improve |
| implement | build / set up / launch |
| operationalize | run |
| in order to | to |
| at this point in time | now |
| it is worth noting | just say it |
| significant opportunity | name the specific gap (and a dollar figure only if the math is honest) |
| competitive landscape | name the competitors |
| several / many / various / numerous | use the actual count |

### Voice
No passive voice. Every sentence has a subject doing something to something else.

**Fails:** "Revenue improvements may be realized by addressing scheduling gaps."
**Works:** "Fixing your Friday schedule could add $14,000 a year."

### Reading level
Write for a 6th-grade reading level. Use the Hemingway App to check if unsure. This is not dumbing down — it is respecting that the reader is busy, tired, and reading on a phone.

## For report findings

A report is **intelligence for an owner — observations, risks, things worth their attention —
not a sales pitch.** The opening sentence is a hook, not a summary. Create tension first.

**Most findings are qualitative, and that's the point.** Do not staple a dollar figure to every
one. A report where every line ends in "$X/yr" reads as AI-generated sales material and the owner
stops trusting it. Quantify *only* where the magnitude is genuinely the point and the math uses real
benchmark numbers — never invented conversion rates or tickets.

**Fails:** "Your business hours are shorter than nearby competitors, which may be limiting revenue during peak demand windows."

**Works:** "Every competitor within half a mile stays open until 10pm on Friday. You close at 8."

Structure:
1. Hook — tension or named gap
2. Consequence — what it costs: **in dollars only if the math is honest, otherwise in concrete
   terms** (a count, a named competitor's edge, a risk)
3. Evidence — the benchmark gap, the competitor, the verbatim quote (this is the proof, dollars or not)
4. Action — specific, with a tool and a timeline
5. Proof — 1-2 verbatim quotes with dates

## For mailer copy

Physical mailers get 3 seconds before someone decides to read or recycle. The headline on page 1 is the only sentence that is guaranteed to be read. Make it do work.

- Lead with a sharp insight or a named competitor (a dollar figure only if it's honest)
- Never open with "We help businesses like yours…"
- The CTA on the last page should be the only logical next step — not one of several options

---

## For website copy

- Assume the visitor is a 45-year-old bar owner reading on their phone between lunch service and dinner prep
- Every section earns its place with a number or a named proof
- Headlines state the outcome, not the process
- "Order a Report" beats "Submit Your Business" — it names what they get, not what they do

---

## For subject lines and cold outreach

- Curiosity gap over feature lists
- Under 8 words when possible
- Never start with "I" or "We"
- The best subject lines feel like the beginning of a sentence the reader needs to finish

**Fails:** "Acme AI — Market Intelligence Reports for Local Businesses"
**Works:** "What the bar across the street is doing that you're not"

---

## What not to do

- Don't summarize before you hook. Start mid-thought.
- Don't use three adjectives when zero would work.
- Don't hedge. "This could potentially lead to possible improvements" is four wasted words. Say what you mean.
- Don't explain your structure ("In this section, we will cover…"). Just cover it.
- Don't end with a whimper. The last sentence of any section should be the strongest one.
