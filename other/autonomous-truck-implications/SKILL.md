# Autonomous Truck Implications

Autonomous trucks have moved from research project to commercial pilot. Some predictions of "driverless freight by 2020" were wrong; the reality of "driverless freight is real but constrained" is now visible. Carriers need to understand what's actually happening and what to plan for — without overreacting or under-reacting.

## SAE levels (relevant to trucks)

| Level | Description | Status in trucks |
|---|---|---|
| Level 0 | No automation | Legacy |
| Level 1 | Driver assist (cruise control) | Universal |
| Level 2 | Partial automation (lane-keep + adaptive cruise) | Standard on new trucks |
| Level 3 | Conditional automation (human takes over when needed) | Limited — legal issues |
| Level 4 | High automation in defined ODD (Operational Design Domain) | Active commercial pilots |
| Level 5 | Full automation, any condition | Not in scope this decade |

Level 4 is the commercial target — a truck that drives itself within defined conditions (highways, daytime, good weather) but requires a human to handle other conditions.

## Active commercial programs (2024–2025)

| Company | Status | Routes |
|---|---|---|
| Aurora Innovation | Commercial driverless ops planned 2025–2026 | TX (Dallas–Houston) initially |
| Kodiak Robotics | Driver-out tests; oil-and-gas + commercial freight | TX, southern US |
| Plus | Level 4 partnership with major OEMs | Various commercial pilots |
| Waymo Via | Significant scale-back of trucking program 2023 | Pivoted to ride-hail focus |
| Embark Trucks | Wound down 2023 (acquired tech by Applied Intuition) | Historical lesson |
| TuSimple | US operations wound down 2024 | China continuing |
| Stack AV | Backed by Softbank; commercial focus | Pilot programs |
| Daimler Truck (autonomous division) | Partnership with Torc Robotics | Production targets late decade |
| Volvo Autonomous Solutions | Confined-area + hub-to-hub focus | Specific applications |
| Locomation | Platooning + autonomous | Active development |

Significant attrition since 2022 — capital has tightened, technical challenges have proven harder than hoped, and survival required clearer paths to revenue.

## The hub-to-hub model (most realistic near-term)

Rather than dock-to-dock autonomous:
1. **Human driver** does first-mile pickup at shipper, drives to "transfer hub" near interstate
2. **Autonomous truck** does the highway middle-mile between hubs
3. **Human driver** does last-mile from receiving hub to consignee

Why this model works:
- Highways are simpler than urban driving
- Hubs can be located on private property (less regulatory friction)
- Humans handle complex pickup/delivery
- Autonomous fleet does long-distance hours not bound by HOS

Aurora, Kodiak, and Daimler are all targeting this model.

## Regulatory status

| Body | Status |
|---|---|
| FMCSA | No specific autonomous-truck rule. Operating under existing CMV rules. |
| NHTSA | Voluntary safety self-assessments for L4. |
| State law | Patchwork. Texas, Arizona explicitly allow. California stricter. |
| Insurance regulators | No standard products yet for L4 commercial trucking |
| Hours of Service | Open question — does L4 count as on-duty for human? |
| CDL requirements | Open question — what driver credential applies to a truck without a driver? |

Federal preemption of state autonomous rules has been proposed but not enacted. Likely 2026–2028 federal framework emerges.

## What "fully autonomous" actually means today

Aurora driverless commercial freight (announced 2024 launch):
- Daytime only
- Texas-only routes initially (TX → AZ planned 2026)
- Light weather (no heavy rain, snow, fog)
- Specific transfer hubs only
- Remote-monitoring of every truck by Aurora Operations Center
- Human "safety driver" still in cab for early commercial runs, transitioning out
- Approximate cost: similar to human-driven for now (premium pricing)

Customer (J.B. Hunt, Schneider, Werner, Uber Freight historically) pays per-load, Aurora provides the truck and operations.

## What carriers should do today

### If you're a small carrier (< 100 trucks)
- Don't invest in autonomous tech
- Watch the hub-to-hub model emerge
- Maintain driver workforce
- Compete on relationships, specialization, geography — areas where AVs lag

### If you're a medium carrier (100–1,000 trucks)
- Pilot one autonomous route via Aurora/Kodiak partnership if available in your lane
- Don't sell your fleet
- Plan workforce evolution (drivers shift to first/last mile)
- Watch insurance market signals

### If you're a large carrier (1,000+ trucks)
- Active partnership discussions with one or more AV providers
- TMS readiness for hybrid AV + human operations
- Driver workforce planning over 10-year horizon
- Engage policy / regulatory work

## Workforce implications

Industry voices are divided. Likely outcomes:
- Long-haul OTR drivers: significant displacement risk over 10–20 years
- Regional, dedicated, LTL P&D drivers: minimal near-term impact
- First/last mile, drayage, vocational: minimal near-term impact
- New roles: AV operations center monitors, remote drivers, transfer-hub drivers

The "driver shortage" narrative becomes more nuanced — long-haul shortage may ease via automation; short-haul / vocational shortage remains acute.

## Insurance and liability

Open questions:
- Who's liable when an L4 truck crashes? OEM? Software vendor? Carrier?
- How is coverage structured?
- What CSA-equivalent measurement applies to autonomous?
- How are crashes recorded against carrier safety record?

Insurance markets are slow to respond. Early adopters likely pay premium pricing or assume more self-insurance.

## Safety record (so far)

Industry data through 2024:
- Autonomous trucks in commercial pilots: very few crashes
- Most incidents low-severity (cones hit, near-miss)
- Per-mile crash rate trending equal or better than human drivers in similar ODD
- BUT: limited mileage, limited conditions — not a fair comparison yet

Eventually data will be definitive. Probably 2026–2028 timeframe.

## Hybrid AV + human models

Some near-term applications:
- Platooning (2 trucks with one driver) — operationally legal in most states
- Convoy (3+ trucks, one driver) — pilot stage
- Driver-assistance leap (handing off to L3 on highway, driver rests/handles paperwork)
- Yard automation (autonomous yard tractors moving trailers in yard)

Yard automation (Outrider, Forterra) is the most-deployed AV application today — confined area, simpler.

## What's slowing adoption

| Barrier | Impact |
|---|---|
| Edge cases (construction, debris, emergency vehicles) | Ongoing engineering challenge |
| Weather (heavy rain, snow, fog) | Sensor limitations |
| Regulatory clarity | State patchwork, federal framework pending |
| Insurance products | Markets still developing |
| Capital cost of AV hardware | $50K–$150K added to truck price |
| Public acceptance | Improving but still mixed |
| Driver workforce concerns | Political and labor pressure |
| Geographic coverage | Hub network must be built |

## Carrier playbook (10-year horizon)

| Year | Action |
|---|---|
| 2025 | Watch commercial deployments; engage with AV provider for pilot conversation |
| 2026 | First commercial deployments at scale in TX/AZ corridors |
| 2027 | Federal regulatory framework likely; insurance products available |
| 2028 | Geographic expansion into Southeast, Midwest |
| 2029 | Reefer + flatbed autonomous deployments begin |
| 2030 | 5–10% of OTR linehaul on autonomous lanes |
| 2032 | 15–25% |
| 2035 | 30–40% |

Predictions vary widely. Track actual commercial launches; ignore unbacked predictions.

## Common mistakes

- Selling driver workforce in expectation of imminent automation — adoption slower than headlines
- Ignoring AV entirely — won't be ready when partnership opportunity arises
- Investing in AV tech directly (not your business) — leave to specialists
- Overconfidence in 10-year timeline (still tons of edge cases unsolved)
- Underestimating regulatory/insurance friction
- Failing to plan driver workforce transition — losing best drivers to competitors
