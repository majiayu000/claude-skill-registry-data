# Microgreens

Microgreens are the fastest path from seed to harvest on a farm — 7 to 21 days from seeding to cut, with no tillage, no season, and minimal space required. Your goal is a consistent, clean harvest that either feeds your household or earns a premium at market. This skill covers the full path: picking the right varieties for your purpose, designing a system you can actually run, seeding and growing without common failures, and troubleshooting when things go wrong.

**How this skill works:** Each sub-tool pauses at a **Checkpoint** to confirm the assumptions it is about to build on before producing output. A recommendation built on a wrong assumption wastes time and money — confirm the checkpoint before acting. Each sub-tool ends with **Next steps** — the skills worth running once you have acted on this one.

## Expert Lineage

*The thinkers whose frameworks underpin this skill — and what they specifically discovered that changes how you farm.*

**Mark Mathew Braunstein — The Microgreen Pioneer**
Braunstein's early work documented microgreens as a distinct horticultural category separate from sprouts — a distinction that matters practically because microgreen production requires a growing medium, light exposure, and root anchorage that sprout production does not. His specific contribution: the observation that cotyledon-stage harvesting captures peak nutritional density in most brassicas before secondary metabolism shifts resources into leaf structure. For the grower, this sets the harvest timing window — wait for the first true leaf on most species, not after.

**Johnny's Selected Seeds Research Programme — Agronomic Benchmarks**
Johnny's conducted systematic trials on seeding density, germination rates, and yield per tray across the major microgreen species. Their most actionable finding: most growers under-seed by 30–40%, producing a thin, leggy crop prone to damping-off and uneven germination. Dense seeding produces mutual support between stems, keeps humidity at root level, and drives the thick, uniform mat that makes professional-quality harvests. Their published seeding rates by species are the best single reference for system calibration.

**Cornell Controlled Environment Agriculture (CEA) Group — Media and Nutritional Research**
Cornell's CEA programme conducted comparative trials on growing media and nutritional outcomes. Their finding that most directly changes practice: soil-grown microgreens — including a mix with biologically active compost — consistently produce higher mineral density than sterile soilless media, particularly for zinc, iron, and calcium. The biological activity in a compost-based medium appears to mediate mineral bioavailability at the root surface even in the very short growing cycle. For growers who care about nutritional density, this makes a strong case for a compost fraction in the medium.

**Elaine Ingham — Soil Food Web (Applied to Short-Cycle Growing)**
Ingham's framework applies here in a limited but real way: even in a 10-day crop, a biologically active growing medium outperforms sterile media for plant health indicators and mineral uptake. Her principle that plants access nutrients through biological mediation — not direct soil chemistry — holds even at microgreen timescales. Practically: a medium with 20–30% finished compost introduces sufficient microbial activity to improve mineral availability, and spent microgreen trays with root mats intact are a higher-value compost input than plain organic matter because they carry an established microbial community.

**Jean-Martin Fortier — Small-Scale Market Production Economics**
Fortier's market garden framework — high value per square metre, direct marketing, crop mix optimisation — applies directly to microgreens at market scale. His specific insight: the crops worth growing for market are those with the highest revenue per unit of time, not per unit of space. Microgreens, priced at $25–50/kg retail, satisfy this test better than almost any field crop. His tracking methodology — revenue per tray, sellout rate, waste percentage — is the management system that separates a profitable microgreen operation from a hobby.

**Charles Dowding — No-Dig Principles (Applied to Tray Systems)**
Dowding's no-dig research on minimal soil disturbance has an analogue in microgreen production: the growers who produce the most consistent harvests are those who treat the tray as a managed biological environment rather than a disposable substrate. His principle that biology, once established, does work the grower does not have to do applies: a medium with active biology handles pH buffering, moisture management, and early pest suppression in ways sterile media cannot. Spent tray material composted and reintroduced into the growing medium creates a biological feedback loop analogous to Dowding's compost-mulch cycle.

## Variety Selection

*Identifies which microgreen species suit your purpose, skill level, and market.*

Not all microgreens are equal to grow. Some species germinate at near 100% with minimal management; others require pre-soaking, blackout periods, or humidity control to produce reliably. Start with the easiest and most marketable before adding difficult species. The framework below lets you match species to your situation.

**Tier 1 — Easy, fast, high market demand:**
These species suit beginners and form the backbone of any market operation.

| Species | Days to harvest | Flavour | Seeding rate (g/10x20 tray) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sunflower | 10–14 | Nutty, mild | 90–120g (hulled) | Pre-soak 8h; thick, satisfying texture |
| Peas | 10–14 | Sweet, fresh | 200–250g | Pre-soak 8–12h; grows tall, popular at market |
| Radish | 7–10 | Peppery, sharp | 25–35g | Fast and reliable; high visual appeal |
| Broccoli | 10–14 | Mild brassica | 20–28g | High sulforaphane — nutritional selling point |
| Mustard | 7–10 | Spicy | 15–20g | Hot varieties (red frills) are visually striking |

**Tier 2 — Medium difficulty, strong market value:**

| Species | Days to harvest | Flavour | Seeding rate (g/10x20 tray) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Amaranth | 10–14 | Mild, earthy | 10–15g | Striking red colour; needs consistent moisture |
| Beet | 12–18 | Earthy, sweet | 35–50g | Soak 4–6h; slow but dramatic colour |
| Corn | 10–16 | Sweet | 120–150g | Pre-soak 6h; needs warmth (21–24°C) |
| Cabbage | 10–14 | Mild brassica | 18–25g | Performs like broccoli; reliable |
| Basil | 14–21 | Aromatic | 8–12g | Needs warmth; mucilaginous seed — do not pre-soak |

**Tier 3 — Specialist or difficult:**
These reward experienced growers but fail unpredictably for beginners.

| Species | Challenge | Worth growing for |
|---|---|---|
| Cilantro/coriander | Slow, uneven germination; pre-crack seeds | Niche market; distinctive flavour |
| Fennel | Slow (18–25 days); needs consistent temp | Chefs' market |
| Wheatgrass | Dense seeding; prone to mould | Juicing market; requires dedicated customer |
| Celery | Tiny seed; very slow; 20+ days | Flavour concentrated; small market |

**For household use:** Radish, broccoli, peas, and sunflower give the best return for the effort. Grow one at a time until the process is reliable.

**For market:** Build a base of three to five Tier 1 species for volume and reliability. Add one or two Tier 2 species for visual interest and premium pricing. Add Tier 3 only when you have a confirmed buyer.

**Checkpoint — confirm before finalising:**
- Household use or market production? The economics of a market operation require species with sufficient margin and volume.
- If market: do you have confirmed buyers, or are you developing the market? Confirmed buyers allow specialisation; market development requires a broader safe range.
- Climate and growing space temperature? Basil and corn require 21–24°C minimum; most other species perform at 16–21°C.

A species recommendation built without knowing the end use produces the wrong variety mix and the wrong seeding density plan.

## System Design

*Designs the physical setup — trays, media, lighting, and space — to match your species list and scale.*

A microgreen system has four physical components: trays, growing medium, light source, and environmental control. Each decision constrains the others. Work through them in sequence.

**1. Tray selection**

The 10x20 inch (25x51 cm) standard nursery flat is the industry default — widely available, stackable, compatible with most accessories. Use two trays per grow: a solid bottom tray (no holes) to hold water and a perforated insert tray for the growing medium.

| Tray approach | When to use | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 10x20 standard stack | Market scale, repeatable production | Most efficient use of shelf space; easy to source |
| Smaller custom trays | Household, variety trialling | Less efficient per unit area; easier to handle individually |
| Shallow (2–3cm depth) | Radish, broccoli, mustard — short-rooted species | Media use is lower; reduce medium cost |
| Deeper (4–5cm depth) | Peas, sunflower — species that root deeply for stem support | Essential for tall species stability |

**2. Growing medium**

Growing medium is the most consequential system decision for nutritional quality.

| Medium | Best for | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Sterile commercial soilless (peat/coir/perlite) | Fast production, mould control, consistent results | Lowest mineral density in the finished crop |
| 70% soilless + 30% finished compost | General purpose — best balance of control and nutrition | Compost source matters — use finished, odour-free compost only |
| 100% finished compost | Nutritional density focus | Higher mould risk; needs careful moisture management |
| Jute or hemp mats (soilless) | Wheatgrass, peas, sunflower — species harvested above the root mat | Cannot be reused; fast and clean |
| Reusable mats (burlap, coconut coir mats) | High-volume operations wanting reduced media cost | Requires cleaning protocol between uses |

*Soil health note: A 20–30% compost fraction introduces biological activity that improves mineral uptake even in the 10–14 day growing window. Spent trays with the root mat intact are a higher-value compost input — add them directly to an active compost pile rather than discarding.*

**3. Light requirements**

| Light source | Appropriate for | Limitations |
|---|---|---|
| South-facing window (4+ hours direct sun) | Household scale, summer production | Insufficient in winter or cloudy climates; leggy growth |
| LED grow lights (full-spectrum, 6500K) | Year-round market production | Capital cost; electricity; heat management |
| T5 fluorescent | Budget start for market scale | Higher electricity cost than LED; bulbs degrade |
| Natural greenhouse / polytunnel light | Seasonal production, medium scale | Supplement in low-light months |

**LED specification for market production:**
- Hang 30–45cm above tray surface during growing phase (after blackout)
- Target 12–16 hours of light per day — use a timer
- Target PPFD of 150–250 µmol/m²/s for most microgreens — do not need the intensity required for fruiting crops
- Full-spectrum (blue + red + white) performs better than red/blue only for flavour and colour development

**4. Space and airflow**

- Minimum ceiling height for shelving with lights: 45cm between shelf levels (tray + 30–45cm hang)
- Air circulation is critical: stagnant humid air is the primary driver of mould. A small fan producing gentle air movement across trays reduces mould incidence dramatically.
- Temperature: 18–22°C is ideal for most species. Below 15°C, germination slows significantly. Above 26°C, risk of damping-off increases.

**5. System scale calculator**

| Goal | Trays per week | Shelf length needed | Light footprint |
|---|---|---|---|
| Household (1–2 meals/week) | 2–4 trays | 1 standard shelf (60cm) | 1 small LED panel |
| CSA supplement / farm shop | 10–20 trays | 1 rack (4 shelves x 120cm) | 2–3 panels |
| Farmers market 1–2 days/week | 30–60 trays | 2–3 racks | 4–6 panels |
| Serious market operation | 80+ trays | Dedicated space with racking system | Purpose-built lighting grid |

**Checkpoint — confirm before finalising:**
- Indoor or outdoor / polytunnel? Outdoor and polytunnel systems need a different light and temperature management approach.
- Is this for year-round production or seasonal? Year-round requires artificial light and heat management.
- Budget range for setup? LED racking for market scale is a real capital investment — confirm the budget before specifying.

Building a system without knowing the light situation and budget produces a design that doesn't match the reality.

## Seeding and Growing

*Step-by-step from seed preparation through to harvest.*

This sub-tool assumes you have chosen your species and designed your system. If not, run **variety-selection** and **system-design** first.

**The production cycle has six stages:**

**Stage 1: Seed preparation (Day 0)**

| Species that need pre-soaking | Soak time | Water temperature |
|---|---|---|
| Sunflower (hulled) | 8 hours | Room temperature |
| Peas (whole dry) | 8–12 hours | Room temperature |
| Corn | 6–8 hours | Room temperature |
| Beet | 4–6 hours | Room temperature |
| Fenugreek | 6 hours | Room temperature |

Do not pre-soak: radish, broccoli, mustard, cabbage, basil (mucilaginous), amaranth. Soaking these increases mould risk without germination benefit.

After soaking, drain and rinse. Do not let soaked seeds sit wet — seed them immediately.

**Stage 2: Seeding (Day 0)**

1. Fill the perforated insert tray with growing medium to 2–3cm depth (or 4–5cm for peas and sunflower). Press lightly to eliminate air pockets. Do not compact — roots need pore space.
2. Moisten the medium until damp throughout but not waterlogged. Squeeze a handful — it should hold shape and release no water when squeezed hard.
3. Distribute seed evenly across the surface. Aim for a single dense layer — seeds touching but not piling. Reference seeding rates in variety-selection above.
4. Press seeds lightly into contact with the medium. A second tray pressed flat on top for 10 seconds works well.
5. Mist the seed surface with clean water.
6. Cover with a blackout dome or inverted tray weighted down.

**Stage 3: Germination under blackout (Days 1–4)**

- Stack trays or apply weight equivalent to 1–2 kg. This presses the seed into contact with the medium and encourages straight, strong stem development.
- Check moisture once daily. Lift the dome and mist if the surface looks dry. Do not waterlog — mist the surface only.
- Remove the dome when the tallest seedlings are pressing against it — typically day 2–4 depending on species and temperature.
- In warm conditions (22°C+), check on Day 2 — fast germinators push domes up quickly and will etiolate if left dark too long.

**Stage 4: Light phase (Days 4–12+, species-dependent)**

- Move trays under light or to a sunny window immediately after dome removal.
- Seedlings will green up within 6–12 hours of light exposure. If they remain pale yellow after 24 hours, light intensity is insufficient.
- Watering: bottom-water where possible. Pour 2–3cm of water into the solid base tray; the perforated insert will absorb from below. This keeps the canopy dry — the leading mould-prevention technique.
- If top-watering is necessary, water at the base of the plants, not over the canopy.
- Water once daily is typical, but monitor — medium should be moist, not saturated or dry.

**Stage 5: Harvest (Days 7–21, species-dependent)**

| Harvest indicator | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Cotyledon stage (recommended for most species) | Both seed leaves fully open and horizontal; first true leaf just beginning to emerge |
| First true leaf (some species — peas, sunflower) | One set of true leaves visible; harvest before second set |
| Taste test | Cut a small sample and taste — peak flavour usually aligns with visual cues |

Harvest method:
- Use clean, sharp scissors or a harvesting knife
- Cut 1–2cm above the medium surface — you want stem, not root mat
- Cut just before use for maximum freshness, or harvest and refrigerate immediately
- Pack loosely — do not compress — in breathable packaging or containers
- Refrigerated shelf life: 5–10 days depending on species. Peas and sunflowers last longest; basil is shortest.

**Stage 6: Post-harvest tray management**

- Spent trays can go directly to a compost pile. The root mat is a high-value compost input — dense with microbial biomass.
- If reusing trays: remove root mat, rinse with water, air dry. Do not reuse medium — start fresh each grow.
- Track each tray: date seeded, species, harvest date, fresh weight. This data drives the variety-selection and system-design decisions over time.

**Checkpoint — confirm before finalising:**
- Have you confirmed the temperature of the growing space? Germination failure is most commonly a temperature problem.
- Is the water source chlorinated? Chlorinated tap water can suppress the compost biology in your medium. Let it sit for 30 minutes before use, or use filtered water.
- Do you have a timer for lights? Manually managing light hours is the fastest route to inconsistency.

Germination failure, leggy growth, and mould are the three most common failures — all are process problems, not species problems. If something goes wrong, go to troubleshooting.

## Troubleshooting

*Diagnoses and resolves the most common microgreen production failures.*

Most microgreen problems fall into four categories: mould, germination failure, poor growth (leggy or pale), and pest problems. Work through the diagnosis below, then apply the targeted fix.

---

**Problem 1: Mould**

Mould is the most common and serious failure mode. White fuzzy growth on the medium surface or stem bases is almost always one of three things: excessive moisture, insufficient airflow, or contaminated medium or seed.

| Symptom | Most likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| White fuzz on medium surface only (not on plants) | Fungal decomposers in medium — usually root hairs, not pathogenic | Not a problem — do not spray. It disappears when trays go under light |
| White fuzz on stem bases with stem collapse | Damping-off (Pythium or Rhizoctonia) — too wet, too warm, no air movement | Cannot save the tray. Identify cause: reduce moisture, add fan, lower temperature |
| Grey-green fuzzy patches (Botrytis) on canopy | Canopy too wet, humidity too high | Switch to bottom-watering. Increase air movement. Harvest earlier |
| Widespread surface mould before germination | Contaminated seed or medium | Source cleaner seed. Use fresh medium. Consider 30-second hydrogen peroxide seed rinse (3% solution) |

Prevention protocol (implement all four):
1. Bottom-water only — keeps canopy dry
2. Fan providing gentle continuous air movement across trays
3. Germination room temperature below 23°C
4. Seed at the correct density — too sparse increases mould risk because canopy doesn't close and moisture sits on the medium surface

---

**Problem 2: Germination failure or uneven germination**

| Symptom | Most likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Nothing germinates after 4 days | Temperature too low (<15°C) | Move to warmer space; use seedling heat mat under trays |
| Patchy germination (50–70% coverage) | Uneven seeding; old seed | Rethink seeding technique; check seed viability |
| Germination starts then stops | Moisture failed during blackout | Check daily and mist as needed |
| Species-specific failure (e.g. cilantro) | Seed needs cracking — hard outer shell | Use cracked coriander seed, or gently crack between two hard surfaces |
| Pre-soaked seed rotting before germination | Over-soaked; anaerobic conditions | Reduce soak time; rinse more thoroughly; seed immediately after soaking |

Seed viability test: Scatter 10 seeds on a damp paper towel, fold, seal in a bag, leave at room temperature for 5 days. Count germination percentage. Below 80% — buy fresh seed.

---

**Problem 3: Leggy, pale, or weak growth**

Leggy growth (long, thin stems) and pale or yellow colour are both light problems. Dense, short, richly coloured growth is the target.

| Symptom | Most likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Long, pale, thin stems | Insufficient light intensity | Move closer to window; increase LED intensity; reduce light distance |
| Yellowing throughout (after dome removal) | Kept under blackout too long; low light | Ensure dome is removed as soon as seedlings push against it |
| Stems lean strongly to one side | Uneven light source | Rotate trays 180° daily; reposition light |
| Short and stocky under dome, then collapses | Medium too wet; damping-off beginning | Reduce watering; improve drainage; use lighter medium |
| Top growth fine but thin stems at base | Seeded too sparsely — mutual stem support missing | Increase seeding density on next tray |

Light troubleshooting: If you suspect light is the problem, measure the distance from the light to the tray surface. For LEDs, the optimal range is 30–45cm. For window growing, the plant needs 4+ hours of direct sun or 6+ hours of bright indirect light — fewer hours than this produces leggy growth regardless of species.

---

**Problem 4: Pest problems**

Microgreens grown indoors have limited pest exposure, but three pests appear commonly.

| Pest | Identification | Control |
|---|---|---|
| Fungus gnats | Small flies at soil surface; larvae in medium eating roots | Yellow sticky traps above trays; diatomaceous earth on medium surface; Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis (Bti) as a drench; bottom-watering reduces surface moisture that draws adults |
| Shore flies | Similar to fungus gnats but faster-moving; less damaging | Same as fungus gnats; improve drainage |
| Thrips | Silver-scarred leaves; tiny insects in leaf folds | Difficult in a microgreen system — harvest early if detected; deep clean all trays and surfaces between grows |
| Aphids | Cluster on new growth | Rinse with water before harvest; deep clean equipment |

The most effective pest prevention in microgreens is sanitation: clean trays between uses, do not allow standing water, and do not let finished or rotting plant material sit near growing trays.

---

**Problem 5: Off flavour or texture**

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Bitter or sour flavour | Harvested too late (true leaf development); heat stress | Harvest earlier; reduce temperature |
| Watery, weak texture | Over-watered; harvested too young | Reduce water; let cotyledons fully develop before cutting |
| Tough or fibrous stems | Species harvested too late | Species-specific — check variety-selection for correct harvest stage |
| Poor shelf life (wilts within 1–2 days) | Harvested at peak heat; not pre-cooled | Harvest in the morning; chill immediately; store at 2–4°C |

**Checkpoint — confirm before finalising:**
- Is this a one-off failure on a single tray, or a recurring pattern across multiple trays? One-off failures are usually process errors; recurring patterns point to a system design problem.
- What is the temperature in the growing space at night? Many failures that seem like day problems are actually night temperature drops.
- When did you last replace your growing medium, and what is its source? Old or contaminated medium causes a surprising proportion of recurring mould and germination problems.

Treating a symptom without confirming the underlying cause produces the same failure on the next tray.
