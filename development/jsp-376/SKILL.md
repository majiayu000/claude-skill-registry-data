# JSP 376: Defence Acquisition Safety Policy

## When to use
**Prerequisites:** none: plain Markdown. Familiarity with ALARP, a safety case, and a business case helps; the glossary defines the Defence-specific terms.

Use this skill for questions about how UK Defence acquisition programmes treat safety: what the SRO and the User each owe, which artefacts a phase produces, how a safety case is owned across the lifecycle, and what evidence an approvals submission carries. It is the acquisition-safety companion to `jsp-815`; it does not replace that pack.

## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### The six concepts
JSP 376 frames acquisition safety through six concepts: Safety Management, Safety Requirements, Test and Evaluation (including Certification), Safety Case, Supervision and Control Activities, and Acquisition Safety Assurance and Approvals Decision Support. The policy defines each concept's key policy statement, then Chapter 3 of the source applies all six to the lifecycle phases.

### Safe to operate and operated safely
The aim of acquisition safety is a capability that is safe to operate and, once in service, is operated safely. Accountability follows the same split: the SRO is accountable through procurement, and ownership moves to the User at entry to service, staying with the User through in-service and disposal. Accountability is personal and cannot be delegated; development and maintenance of safety work can be assigned, but the SRO/User remains answerable for it.

### The seven-phase lifecycle and the three business cases
Acquisition runs through Pre-Concept plus the six CADMID/T phases: Concept, Assessment, Demonstration, Manufacture, In-Service, and Disposal/Termination. Each phase executes the plan agreed in the previous phase, reviews the outcome, and plans what remains. Formal approving-authority decisions fall at the end of the first three phases, each on a business case: the Strategic Outline Case at the end of Pre-Concept, the Outline Business Case at the end of Concept, and the Full Business Case at the end of Assessment. The greatest opportunity to influence safety is early, and late mitigation costs more in performance, cost, and time.

### Must, must make sure, and should
The policy's three directive terms carry different weights. Where it says must, the action is a compulsory requirement completed by the actioner. Where it says must make sure, delivery may be assigned to an individual, but the actioner remains accountable for the actions being conducted. Where it says should, the action is not compulsory but is recommended good practice.
