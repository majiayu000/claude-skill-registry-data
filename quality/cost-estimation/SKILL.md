# Cost Estimation

Numbers before money is committed: estimate from elements, state the class and range, and track every estimate against its actual — that's how estimates get good.

## When to use
- Predicting cost of work, equipment, or purchases before committing.
- Reviewing vendor quotes (should-cost).
- Building rates and reference data from history.

## Method
1. **Class the estimate**: concept/ROM (wide range, ±30–50% reality), budget (±15–20%), definitive (±5–10%) — label it, so nobody treats ROM as a quote.
2. **Method per situation**: bottom-up (elements) when design exists; parametric (unit rates, cost curves) when history exists; analogous (adjust a comparable explicitly) for one-offs.
3. **Elements, not lump sums**: material, labor (hours × rates incl. burden), equipment, subcontract, freight, indirects — each line traceable.
4. **Uncertainty with three points**: optimistic/likely/pessimistic for shaky items → expected value + spread; ranges always, precision theater never.
5. **Escalation & currency**: inflation/fx/lead-time impacts stated; quote validity dates captured.
6. **Should-cost**: for big items, build an independent benchmark and compare against vendor quotes; reconcile gaps (scope? rates? margin?).
7. **Quote discipline**: review exclusions and assumptions line by line; a quote without exclusions is a trap; validity period respected.
8. **Feedback loop**: track estimate vs actual per item; update rates; publish variance lessons (that's the compounding skill).

## Quality gates
- [ ] Basis document: class, method, assumptions, exclusions, estimator.
- [ ] Ranges (not points) for material uncertainty; escalation handled.
- [ ] Quotes' exclusions reviewed; divergences explained.
- [ ] Estimate-vs-actual tracked and fed back into rates.

## Red lines
- No single-number precision on uncertain scope; no vendor quote accepted without scope review; no padding hidden inside rates.
