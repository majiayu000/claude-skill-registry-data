# Draft

Structure is settled; build the prose that serves it. Grow one unit at a time,
keeping the reader's footing at every step — the two failure modes of drafting
are writing ahead of the user and writing ahead of the reader.

## The loop

1. **Read the inputs in full**: the outline contract (spine or story engine),
   the bible where one exists, the fragment pile. State the spine — the claim
   the piece argues, or the story engine (want, obstacle, dramatic question) —
   so every block has something to serve.
2. **Set the reader's starting point.** List the concepts or facts the reader
   is assumed to hold (**prerequisites**). Everything else must be grounded
   inside the piece before a later block leans on it. This one lever decides
   who the piece is for: demand too much up front and you shut readers out;
   ground too much inside and the opening drowns in setup.
3. **Grow one unit at a time.** Before each unit, answer: what does the reader
   need next that the spine demands? That answer chooses the unit. Write only
   the unit in hand — the user may redirect at any seam, and nothing written
   ahead of a decision survives the redirect.
4. **Choose the form deliberately and say why**: scene vs summary, prose vs
   list, paragraph vs table (fiction moves in [references/fiction-scenes.md](references/fiction-scenes.md); nonfiction forms in
   [references/block-forms.md](references/block-forms.md)).
5. **Write to the file immediately** when the unit is agreed. Re-read the file
   from disk before every write — the user may have edited between turns.
   Change only the block in hand; never overwrite user text.
6. **Stop when the spine is made and carried** — the claim proven, or the
   dramatic question answered and the exit state reached. Sentence polish is
   `writing-edit`'s; diagnosis is `writing-critique`'s.

At real forks — the opening, a scene's approach, a pivot the outline left
open — write 2–3 candidate versions and let the user pick. Candidates at
junctures are how the draft stays the user's; outside junctures, just write.

## Mining the source

Treat outlines, fragment piles, and transcripts as a quarry, not a script:
split, merge, rework, and rephrase fragments into one voice. The pile is there
to be mined.

**Name gaps; don't paper over them.** When the piece needs something the
material does not contain — an example, a scene, a number, a reaction — stop
and say so: "this scene needs her refusal and we don't have one; give me one
now or we cut it." Never invent a fact, a quote, or a story event to bridge a
gap; inventing is how drafts quietly go wrong and why bibles exist.

## Fiction branch

Scenes, not explanation. Read
[references/fiction-scenes.md](references/fiction-scenes.md) before the first
fictional scene: scene/sequel rhythm, interiority and narrative distance,
dialogue as action with subtext, and the exposition moves that keep the reader
oriented without an info-dump. The non-negotiables:

- Every scene has a **turn** — the exit state differs from the entry state.
- Connect with **therefore / but**; two _and then_ scenes in a row means one is
  dead.
- **Grounding discipline**: no concept, term, place, or fact of the world may
  be used before the prose has established it — and every newly established
  fact goes back to the bible in the same session.
- Interiority is the point: what it's like to be this person now. Summary
  reports events; scenes put the reader behind the eyes.

## Nonfiction branch

One claim per block; the block states it, supports it, and stops. Evidence
before adjectives. Transitions by logic — _therefore_, _but_ — rather than by
sequence — _also_, _next_ — so the piece reads as an argument, not a list of
adjacent facts.

## Out of scope

Fixing sentences that already work, reordering settled structure without
flagging it, editing the bible, or publishing-format concerns the user didn't
raise.
