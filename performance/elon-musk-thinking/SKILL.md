# Elon Musk Method of Thinking

Two pillars: **first-principles reasoning** + a ruthless engineering **algorithm**. The discipline is in the *order* — most teams optimize and automate things they should have deleted.

## The Algorithm (5 steps — order matters)
1. **Make the requirements less dumb.** Question every requirement; each must carry a *person's name*, not a department. Even a smart person's requirements are partly dumb — so interrogate them.
2. **Delete the part or process.** If you're not occasionally forced to add ~10% back, you didn't delete enough. "The best part is no part; the best process is no process."
3. **Simplify and optimize** — but only *after* deleting. The classic error is optimizing something that shouldn't exist.
4. **Accelerate cycle time** — speed up, but only after the first three. Never accelerate something you should have deleted.
5. **Automate** — last. Automating before deleting and simplifying bakes the waste in permanently.

## First principles, not analogy
Boil to fundamentals (physics, material costs) and build up. Use the **idiot index** — finished-component cost ÷ raw-material cost — to find where you're paying for inefficiency rather than physics. A high index is an opportunity.

## Operating corollaries
- Technical managers must have hands-on knowledge — don't manage what you don't understand.
- Beware excess comradery: it makes people stop flagging problems. Make it safe to say "this is wrong."
- Physics is the law; expert opinion and convention are recommendations.
- Set near-impossible targets to *force* first-principles redesign; tolerate risk; iterate fast on prototypes (fail fast where failure is cheap and reversible).
- Weigh efforts by probability-of-success × importance; take a long time horizon.

## Safety-critical caution (important for Nrupal)
The deletion bias is powerful but dangerous in safety-critical systems (his electrical/protection domain). **Delete dumb requirements; never delete a protection layer that physics or regulation requires.** The "add 10% back" rule exists precisely because over-deletion happens — respect it.
